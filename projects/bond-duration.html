Objective

I started this project to build a practical understanding of interest-rate risk: how much bond prices move when rates change, and how that sensitivity differs across Treasuries and corporate bonds.

Work completed

Original work (summer 2026)

I began a self-study Excel analysis of Treasuries and corporate bonds, calculated duration, and attempted ±100-basis-point rate scenarios.

Corrected synthetic exercise

AI later helped correct and complete the published exercise, which uses hypothetical inputs for eight bonds and adds exact repricing comparisons.

Scope and methodology

Eight hypothetical fixed-coupon bonds: five Treasury-style (2- to 30-year) and three corporate (two A-rated, one BBB), priced to a settlement date of 1 October 2026.

  • Pricing: Excel's PRICE function with $100 face value, semiannual coupons, and the US 30/360 day count, a simplification of actual Treasury conventions.
  • Duration: Macaulay duration from discounted cash-flow timing, adjusted for settlement between coupon dates (COUPNUM, COUPDAYBS, COUPDAYS). Modified duration = Macaulay ÷ (1 + yield ÷ 2).
  • Duration estimate: price change ≈ −modified duration × change in yield × dirty price.
  • Exact repricing: each bond is repriced with PRICE at the shocked yield.
  • Limits: the estimate ignores convexity. Credit spreads, default, and portfolio weights are not modeled.

Findings

From the synthetic workbook · verified with AI assistance
  1. Direction. All eight synthetic examples fall in price when yields rise and rise when yields fall.
  2. Sensitivity. The UST 30Y example has the highest modified duration (16.0751 years) and is the most sensitive in proportional terms: about −14.4% at +100 bp and +18.2% at −100 bp, based on exact repricing.
  3. Linear estimate vs exact repricing. Duration is a linear approximation, but the price–yield relationship is curved (convexity). For the UST 30Y, exact repricing gives a smaller loss and a larger gain than the duration estimate.
